How this works

This page explains, in plain terms, how a fact gets from an official government document onto this site — and where to look when something is disputed or missing.

From official record to this page

Every fact on this site starts as a document published by an official source: LegiScan's bulk data service and the Missouri Senate's own journals for Missouri Senate roll calls, the house.mo.gov XML data service for Missouri House bills and votes, and Congress.gov, the Clerk of the U.S. House, and the U.S. Senate's Legislative Information System for federal bills and votes. Nothing on this site is entered by hand from a news story, a press release, or anyone's recollection of what happened.

Before an individual legislator's recorded vote ("cast") on a roll call is stored, the count of casts for each position — yea, nay, not voting, absent — must match the tally the source itself printed for that roll call. If the counts don't match, nothing is quietly corrected or averaged to make them fit: the mismatched roll call is held back, or, when two independent sources genuinely disagree about the same roll call, it is flagged for a human to read both sources and decide (see "Reconciliation flags and quarantined data" below). A cast is never invented to make a tally add up.

A bill's plain-language summary is authored only from that bill's own official text — never from a model's general knowledge of the subject, and never from news coverage or commentary about the bill. Before a summary publishes, a second, independent model reads the same official text and tries to find anything the summary claims that the text does not actually support — an adversarial check, not a second opinion that simply agrees.

A summary publishes automatically only after it clears mechanical completeness checks (every required section present, every claim's citation resolves to real text in the source) and the adversarial verification comes back clean. The system never trusts a model's own report that it verified something; the check itself has to run and produce a clean result. Anything the verification flags waits in a queue for this project's maintainer to read personally before it can publish — a flagged item is never quietly waved through, and the page says so wherever it applies.

Every published fact carries its own receipt: a link to the official source, and, where the fact is drawn from a larger document, the exact quoted excerpt it came from. Nothing here asks you to trust a rating or a label in place of the source.

Reconciliation flags and quarantined data

A reconciliation flag means two of this project's independent data sources recorded the same roll call differently — for example, LegiScan's tally and the Missouri Senate's own printed journal disagree about how a specific legislator voted, or about the final count. When that happens, the disagreement is not resolved by picking whichever number looks right, or by averaging the two. It is recorded as an open flag and set aside for a human to read both sources and decide. Any vote shown on this site carrying a "⚑ reconciliation flag" badge links back to this page.

While a flag is open, this site does not blend or split the difference between the disagreeing sources — it displays the record from this project's designated primary source for that chamber, shown exactly as printed. This is primary-source-first display: the badge is the reader's signal that a second source disagrees, so you can go compare both original documents yourself, rather than trust a number this site invented to reconcile them.

Missouri Senate roll-call detail — which legislator cast which vote — comes from scanned journal documents that have to be mechanically extracted before they can be checked against LegiScan's own tallies. When a given journal day's extraction cannot be automatically confirmed against the printed tally (a parsing failure, an unreadable page, a mismatch that cannot be resolved without a person looking at the source), that day is quarantined: held out of the published record rather than guessed at, and counted honestly as a day "pending human review," never silently dropped and never silently published as if it had been confirmed. The same discipline runs in both directions: a fact is never invented to fill a gap, and a fact is never suppressed just because it is inconvenient. A dispute or a gap is shown as a dispute or a gap, with a path back to the primary documents so it can be checked directly.

Attribution

This site republishes and summarizes records published by the sources below. The license notices are stated because their terms require it, and because anything on this site should be traceable back to where it actually came from.

  • LegiScan (legiscan.com) — Missouri roll-call and bill data, used under LegiScan's CC BY 4.0 license, which requires this attribution wherever the data is shown.
  • Congress.gov (congress.gov) — federal bill text, status, and sponsor data.
  • U.S. Senate Legislative Information System (LIS) (senate.gov/legislative/LIS) — U.S. Senate roll-call votes.
  • house.mo.gov XML data service (documents.house.mo.gov/xml) — Missouri House bill and roll-call data.
  • Missouri Senate (senate.mo.gov), including the Senate's own published journals — the primary source this project's Missouri Senate roll-call reconciliation checks against. Same disclaimer as the bill page: "Bill text and status shown are not official copies; the official record is at the linked source." The journal data on this site is drawn from those published journals but is not itself an official copy — the official record is the Missouri Senate's own published journal.
  • U.S. Census Bureau Geocoder (geocoding.geo.census.gov/geocoder), used by the "Find your representatives" address lookup. Restated verbatim from that page: "Your address is sent to the U.S. Census Bureau geocoder to find your districts, and is not stored or logged — not by us, not ever."
  • unitedstates/congress-legislators (github.com/unitedstates/congress-legislators), released under a CC0 (public domain) dedication. Adopted as this site's source for federal legislators' real, per-term start and end dates.
  • Photographs — sourced from the Congressional Biographical Directory (bioguide.congress.gov) and served via the unitedstates/images mirror (unitedstates.github.io/images/congress).
